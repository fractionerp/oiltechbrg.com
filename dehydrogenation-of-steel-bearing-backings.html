A whitemetal (Babbitt) bearing depends on the bond between its lining and the steel backing. The lining is soft by design. It relies on a continuous metallurgical bond, formed through a tinned layer, to carry load and conduct heat into the backing.

Hydrogen held in the steel is one of the less visible threats to that bond. At Oiltech, every steel backing for a new whitemetal bearing goes through a dehydrogenation heat treatment before it is tinned and lined. Not every supplier does this. This article explains why we do it, how it is done and what it prevents.

Where the hydrogen comes from

Hydrogen dissolves in steel as individual atoms. They are small enough to diffuse through the iron lattice and collect at inclusions, defects and internal voids. Steel for a bearing backing can pick up hydrogen at several stages before it reaches the lining shop:

  • Steelmaking. Moisture and hydrogen-bearing compounds in the charge and furnace atmosphere dissolve in molten steel. Steelmakers work hard to remove hydrogen, but some can remain, particularly in heavier sections.
  • Casting and forging. Cast and forged backings in larger sections can retain hydrogen from the melt. Heavy forgings are routinely given hydrogen-removal heat treatments by steelmakers for this reason.
  • Pickling and chemical cleaning. Acid cleaning generates atomic hydrogen at the steel surface, and some of it diffuses into the metal instead of escaping as gas. Surface preparation is an essential part of making a good bond, so the steps that clean the steel can also add hydrogen to it.

None of this means the steel is defective. Levels that cause no problem in a structural part can still matter at a bond line that has to be continuous over the whole bore.

Why hydrogen matters at the bond line

Hydrogen solubility in steel at room temperature is very low, and hydrogen diffuses much faster as steel is heated. Tinning and lining heat the backing well above ambient: fluxing and tinning are carried out at several hundred degrees Celsius, and the whitemetal is cast molten against the hot tinned surface.

So any hydrogen still in the backing tends to come out exactly when the tin layer is forming and the lining is solidifying. Gas evolving at the interface can:

  • cause porosity in the tinned layer or at the bond line;
  • stop the tin from wetting the steel continuously, leaving unbonded areas;
  • form blisters under the lining;
  • leave voids in the whitemetal close to the interface.

What it prevents

Failed bond tests. Ultrasonic testing to ISO 4386-1 detects areas where the lining is not bonded to the backing. A bearing that fails is caught before it ships, but it costs time and has to be re-lined.

In-service delamination. The more serious case is a marginal bond that passes inspection, or a defect sitting in an area the ultrasonic test cannot assess, such as near edges, joint faces and oil grooves. In service, hydrodynamic load and thermal cycling can make small unbonded areas grow. Oil can get under a loose lining and help lift it, and an unbonded patch conducts heat poorly. The result can be cracking, delamination and loss of lining, which means premature bearing failure and unplanned downtime on the pump, turbine, compressor or gearbox.

Removing the hydrogen before tinning deals with the cause instead of relying only on inspection to catch the effect.

How Oiltech dehydrogenates steel backings

Dehydrogenation is part of our original manufacturing route for steel-backed whitemetal bearings. Before tinning, every steel backing is heat treated in a furnace at above 600 °C for around 12 hours minimum. The cycle runs in a temperature-controlled furnace with a steady ramp up and a long hold at the required temperature, so heat reaches right through the section and the hydrogen has time to diffuse out.

The principle is straightforward. Hydrogen diffusivity rises steeply with temperature, so a sustained hold at elevated temperature gives dissolved hydrogen time to diffuse out of the full section and escape. The backing then goes on to cleaning, tinning and lining.

After lining, our bearings are centrifugally cast and ultrasonically bond tested to ISO 4386-1. Dye penetrant testing to ISO 4386-3 is available for exposed bond lines on end and joint faces.

Questions to ask your bearing supplier

If you buy whitemetal bearings, these are reasonable questions to put to any supplier:

  • Are steel backings dehydrogenated before tinning, as standard on every bearing?
  • What is the heat treatment cycle, and is it recorded?
  • Is every bearing bond tested to ISO 4386-1, and to which test class?
  • Are inspection records supplied with the bearings?

FAQ

Is dehydrogenation the same as stress relieving?

Not exactly. The purpose of dehydrogenation is to remove hydrogen. However, a cycle above 600 °C falls in the temperature range commonly used for sub-critical stress relief of carbon steels, so it also provides stress relief in the steel. That is a benefit in its own right in bearing shell manufacture, because it reduces residual stresses from earlier machining or fabrication and helps the shell stay dimensionally stable during final machining.

Doesn't the heat of tinning drive the hydrogen out anyway?

That is the problem. Tinning drives hydrogen out at the wrong moment, while the bond is forming, which is how porosity and unbonded areas start. The aim is to remove it before tinning begins.

Will an ultrasonic bond test catch every defect?

ISO 4386-1 testing is an essential check, but it distinguishes only bond from bond defect, not bond strength, and some zones such as edges, joint faces, oil holes and grooves cannot be assessed. Preventing defects through process control is better than relying on inspection alone.
